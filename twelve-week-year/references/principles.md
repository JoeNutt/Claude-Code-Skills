# The Three Principles and the Emotional Cycle of Change

The disciplines are what you do. The principles are the stance that makes the disciplines
stick. See `sources.md` for provenance.

---

## Accountability — ownership, not consequences

- *"Accountability is not consequences, it's ownership."* It is a character trait and a life
  stance, not something done to you.
- **We always have choice.** *"There are no have-to's in life."* *"The quality of our choices
  determines the quality of our life."*
- **We control only two things: our thinking and our actions.** Stop looking outside
  yourself for the cause.
- **Hold people capable, not accountable.** Scrub the notion of "holding others
  accountable". In the WAM, participants are accountable to themselves.

Understood this way, accountability is freeing rather than threatening — it puts control
back with the person.

## Commitment — promises to yourself, kept

Commitment is *"the state of being bound emotionally or intellectually to some course of
action"* — a personal promise. Accountability looks at what you own now; commitment
projects it into the future.

Four keys to a successful commitment — **wording unverified against the book**, from
reader highlights and summaries:

1. **Strong desire** — a clear, personally compelling reason.
2. **Keystone actions** — the one or two core activities that produce most of the result.
3. **Count the costs** — know in advance what keeping it will take.
4. **Act on commitments, not feelings** — do it whether or not you feel like it.

**The Field Guide's commitment worksheet** (Ch 3) — for personal breakthroughs outside the
plan's goals: write a positively stated goal; pick the one commitment action with the
greatest impact, ideally daily or weekly; list its costs; commit only to those whose price
you are willing to pay. Areas: spiritual, family/spouse, physical/health, business/career,
financial, personal, community/service. Commitments are 12 weeks long — far easier to keep
than annual or lifetime ones. Pitfalls: missing once and giving up; failing to confront a
missed commitment; not valuing your word.

## Greatness in the Moment

- *"Life is lived in the moment: greatness happens in the moment."*
- **Results are the confirmation of greatness, not the attainment.** You become great the
  moment you choose to do what needs doing — long before the result arrives.
- The tendency is to plan to be great *later*. There is only now.
- The difference between great and mediocre on any one day or week is small. It compounds.
- *"Too many people choose comfortable activity over pleasurable results. Be willing to
  sacrifice your comfort and you will be great."*

## The Emotional Cycle of Change

Adapted by the authors from Kelley and Conner. Every change passes through five phases:

| Phase | Name | What it feels like |
|---|---|---|
| I | **Uninformed Optimism** | The initial high. Benefits are clear, often overestimated; costs are unknown. |
| II | **Informed Pessimism** | Emotion, energy and enthusiasm drop fast. Costs are now clear; benefits feel out of reach. |
| III | **The Valley of Despair** ("the Wall") | Where most people and organisations quit — and many cycle through I–III repeatedly, training themselves to never finish. |
| IV | **Informed Optimism** | Pushed through. Benefits begin to materialise; a realistic view of both challenge and reward. |
| V | **Success & Fulfilment** | The results are realised. |

**Why it matters.** The valley is predictable, not a sign the plan is wrong. Two things carry
you through it:

- **Vision** — without a compelling why, it is too easy to retreat to the comfortable and
  familiar.
- **Process control** — the weekly routine keeps you working the plan when motivation is gone.

When the user is in Phase II or III: name the phase, reconnect them with the vision, and
point at the next tactic due this week — not at a new plan.
