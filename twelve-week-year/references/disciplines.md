# The Five Disciplines

In the authors' order. Quotations are from the authors' own material — see `sources.md`.

---

## 1. Vision

**Purpose.** Vision is the emotional and intellectual *why*. *"Most change fails because
the price required is too high. The ultimate price of change? Your comfort."* A vision you
are unwilling to abandon is what makes you willing to pay that price. Vision is the first
place results are either expanded or limited.

**Size.** *"Big thinking always precedes big achievement."* A vision should stretch, and
should feel uncomfortable. If it is achievable without doing anything differently, it is
too small.

**Sequence.**

1. **Have–Do–Be** (~20 min). Three columns: everything you want to *have* (material and
   not), to *do*, to *be*. Stretch past the comfortable. This is a brainstorm, not the
   vision — items that appear in more than one column tend to make the final cut.
   Suspend "how will I do this?"; ask only "what if?".
2. **Long-term vision** — 5, 10, 15+ years. Draw from Have–Do–Be. Commit to it.
3. **Three-year vision, personal** — note your age three years from now. Cover spouse,
   family, health, spiritual, social, financial, intellectual, emotional, lifestyle.
4. **Three-year vision, professional** — it must *align with and fund* the personal vision,
   give the desired free time, and be rewarding in itself. Ideal role, strengths, value
   created, income, time off, whether you lead others.
5. **12-month vision** (optional) — where you must be in four 12 Week Years to be on track
   for the three-year vision.

The three-year vision is *"a stake in the ground"*: time-bound and more specific than the
long-term one; it defines what greatness looks like three years from today.

**Three pitfalls.**

- *Not taking it seriously* — treating vision as fluff and leaping to action. Symptom: not
  keeping it in front of you, not aligning plans with it, not remembering what is in it.
- *Not making it meaningful* — writing what you think you should want. Keep working until
  there is an emotional connection.
- *Making it too small* — achievable, but leaves your best undelivered.

**Four actions to keep it alive.** Share it with someone who matters. Review it every
morning. Connect it to daily actions. End each day reflecting on whether today moved it
forward, and resolve what tomorrow's action is.

## 2. Planning

**Purpose.** *"If your Vision is the 'what,' then your 12 Week Plan is the 'how.'"*
Planning is problem-solving: how to get from here to there. What makes 12-week planning
different: greater predictability, increased focus, and a structure built for
implementation.

**Two tiers.**

- **12-week goals** — outcomes: where you will be at the end of week 12. Linked to the
  vision. One to three, *"less is more"*. Phrase: *"For the 12 Week Year ending
  dd/mm/yyyy, I have …"*.
- **Tactics** — actions that achieve each goal, each with a due week.

**Five criteria for a goal.**

| Criterion | Test |
|---|---|
| Specific & measurable | Can you define where "there" is? If you can't quantify it, qualify it — what will be different in 12 weeks. |
| Stated positively | Words create focus. "98% accuracy", not "2% error rate". "Become a non-smoker", not "quit smoking". |
| Realistic stretch | Reachable without change → not enough. Cold sweat → factor it back. |
| Accountability | Put nothing in the plan you are unwilling to own. |
| Time bound | Bound by the 12 weeks, and every tactic has a due week. |

**From goal to tactics.**

1. **Mind map** the goal: write it in a circle, brainstorm every possible action around
   it. Push through at least three waves of stalling.
2. **Select the critical few.** Ask *"If I could only do one thing on this list, which would
   have the greatest impact?"* Circle it. Ask again. Stop as soon as the circled set is
   enough. If two will do, don't take three.
3. **Write each as tactics.** A mind-map item is a concept, and often becomes several
   tactics. *"A tactic is a sentence, typically starts with a verb, and describes an action
   that you can take."* Write the actual action — "call", "email", "draft" — never
   "communicate". The Field Guide's four tactic criteria:
   1. Conforms to the criteria for well-written 12-week goals.
   2. Starts with an action verb and is written as a complete sentence.
   3. Can be executed as written, in the week it is due, without significant prework.
   4. Specifies frequency and due dates — e.g. *"Run three times/week for a minimum of
      three miles/run, weeks 2–12."*

**Barriers.** Before finalising, name the top two or three internal and external things
that could stop the plan (a holiday, distraction, short staffing) and plan around them —
for example, no tactics due in a holiday week.

**Support.** For each goal, note the support or assistance needed from others.

**There is no perfect plan.** The only way to know whether a plan works is to execute it
and measure it. Planning is a skill; the next plan will be better written.

## 3. Process Control

**Purpose.** *"Process control is a set of tools and events that you can lean on when you
don't feel 'motivated' to do what you need to do."* It is how top performers are
disciplined: they build in process rather than relying on willpower. It exists to carry
you through the emotional cycle of change (see `principles.md`).

**The Weekly Execution Routine (WER)** — three steps, in this order, about 20 minutes a
week. *"Easy to do, and even easier not to do."* Tactics come due in a **week**, not on a
day: *"the week is the crucible for execution."*

1. **Score last week** — see Scorekeeping. Do it before the new week begins.
2. **Write this week's plan.** Build the Weekly Plan: the tactics due this week from the
   12-week plan, overdue ones still relevant, and weekly commitments. It is *"a 1/12 slice
   of the 12 Week Plan"* — it does not contain everything you do, only the plan items.
   *"Not a glorified 'to-do' list."* Pitfalls: not planning each week; including all your
   tasks; copying last week's plan; adding tactics weekly — a new tactic goes into the
   12-week plan first, then flows down. Check the plan first thing, during the day, and
   before finishing.
3. **Peer support — the WAM** (Weekly Accountability Meeting).
   - Monday morning, after everyone has planned their week and scored the last one.
   - ~15 minutes, never more than 30. Groups of 3–4. In person or by phone.
   - Agenda — each person reports: **results** against goal (where you are vs where you
     should be); **execution** (last week's score); **intentionality** (what you will be
     intentional about this week). Then encourage and close.
   - Not a problem-solving or strategy session. Not punitive: *"we hold each other
     capable."*
   - Anyone who has struggled for two weeks or more is asked what they will do differently
     this week and whether the group can help. *"In a 12 Week Year you cannot afford many
     'bad' weeks."*
   - In the first WAM, each member shares their plan and why the goal matters to them.
   - Field Guide agenda: individual report-out (results actual vs goal; weekly execution
     score; what's working, where I'm struggling, action commitment this week) → group
     challenges, feedback, encouragement → close and follow-ups. Agree WAM rules up front.

**Other process-control tools** (layer them in; don't start with all five): a **Daily
Huddle** — five minutes, standing, at most three people: yesterday's accomplishments and
today's intentions; a **12 Week Theme** drawn from the plan that motivates ("Own It");
and a **celebration** decided in advance as the reward for the 12 weeks.

## 4. Scorekeeping

**Purpose.** *"Measurement drives the process. It is the anchor of reality."* Without
measurement you cannot make intelligent mid-course adjustments. People shy away from
measurement; treat it as feedback, not judgment.

**Lead and lag.** Lag indicators are the end results. Lead indicators are early measures
of progress toward them. Pick the top one or two of each per goal, with a weekly target
and a 12-week target, and track them weekly — *"a week lost is a month lost."*

**The execution score is the most powerful lead indicator.** It measures whether you did
what you said was most important: *tactics completed ÷ tactics due this week*.

- *"What you are scoring is not your results, but your execution."*
- Field Guide: *"if you accomplish an average of 80% or more of your weekly tactics over
  the 12 Week Year, then, in most cases, you will reach your goal."* (The Academy
  material says 85%; the Field Guide and the 2020 Leader Guide say 80%. Use 80%.)
- *"If this stuff gets done you had a great week. If it doesn't, you lost a week."*
- It will be uncomfortable; you won't always score well. Have the courage to score anyway.
- Under 80% isn't automatically bad: 65% may be an improvement. Commit to progress each
  week — 45% to 55% is a win.

Track results daily, weekly and monthly; score execution weekly.

**The four weekly execution scenarios** — the score read against the lead and lag:

| Scenario | Score | Lead/lag | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ≥80% | on track | Keep executing. Don't get complacent. |
| 2 | <80% | off track | **Don't change the plan** — you don't know if it works yet. Find the tactics being avoided and recommit. Ask: is the weekly plan with you, checked several times a day? What are you doing instead of working the plan? |
| 3 | ≥80% | off track | The plan isn't working. If the score isn't inflated and the lead measures have had time, change the plan — more activity or better technique. |
| 4 | <80% | on track | Luck (raise the goal or discount the windfall), a plan harder than needed (right-size it), or a different plan being worked (write that one down instead). |

**Confront the truth** (Field Guide Ch 8, optional): each week record last week's score
and the average to date; each lead and lag measure as last week vs weekly target and
cumulative vs cumulative target; then trends, risks and gaps, performance breakdowns
(name the tactics being avoided), and action commitments for this week. Monthly, every
four weeks, if weekly is too much.

## 5. Time Use

**Purpose.** *"If you are not in control of your time, then you are not in control of your
results."* *"Ultimately, personal effectiveness is about intentionality."*

**Blue chips and white chips.** Hard work does not guarantee results; hard work on the right
things does. Tasks differ in value. And *you cannot get it all done* — once that is
accepted, prioritising becomes unavoidable.

- **White chips** (low value): stop doing them, delegate them (to a person or a system), or
  make them more efficient.
- **Blue chips** (high value): identify one to three that would most move performance and
  make room for them.

**Performance Time** — time blocking.

| Block | What | Starting cadence |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategic** | 3 hours, scheduled in advance, uninterrupted — no calls, email, visitors. Blue-chip and strategic work: reconnect with the vision, review progress and execution, assess breakdowns, execute strategic tactics, build the next plan. | 1 per week |
| **Buffer** | Pre-set time for the unplanned and low-value: email, calls, interruptions. Redirect interruptions to it ("I'm free 11–12 or 4–5"). Works for 60–70% of interruptions. | 1–2 per day, 30–60 min |
| **Breakout** | 3+ hours during normal working hours with no work activity or thought. Refresh. *Earned* by keeping the strategic and buffer blocks — without them performance suffers. | ~1 per month to start |

**Model Week.** Block an ideal week: the three block types, routine tasks on the same day
and time each week, important work in the hours you are sharpest, and enough white space for
the unexpected.

**The four constructive beliefs** (Field Guide Ch 6): your time is at least as valuable as
the time of others; you can't get everything done; work on the high-priority,
results-generating activity first; breakthrough requires breakout from your old systems.
Schedule the strategic block early in the week, so an interrupted one can be rebuilt the
same week, and in the slot with the least activity.

**The barrier is thinking, not mechanics.** "I have an open-door policy", "I can't focus
for three hours", "too many interruptions" — these are thinking barriers. Don't get hung up
on the mechanics and miss the concept.

## The 13th week

Between cycles. Celebrate, capture the learning, launch again. The Field Guide's review
(Ch 7) takes a two-hour block with the plan and every weekly plan to hand:

1. **Results and execution** — each goal rated 1 (no real progress) to 10 (accomplished);
   the twelve weekly scores and their average; what worked and what didn't; if the
   average was under 80%, what will be different.
2. **Quality of life** — spiritual, spouse/key relationship, family, business, personal,
   physical, each rated 1–10 with a trend. Aim for *intentional imbalance*, not balance.
   What am I pleased with; what will I improve next.
3. **Success disciplines** — vision, 12-week planning, weekly routine, performance time,
   key measures, each rated 1–10; which to use better next time.
4. **Breakthroughs** — the most significant accomplishment; how thinking would need to
   change to double the results; what new actions that takes.

Then take the celebration, revise the vision, and build the next plan without losing
momentum.
